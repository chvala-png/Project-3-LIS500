A Teachable Machines Project

Our Project Statement

Intro

For our project, we created a hand classification algorithm. This was done using Teachable Machine by Google. We created this to develop a machine learning model that could recognize different hand gestures including: thumbs up, thumbs down, heart sign, peace sign, and rock-on sign. Throughout the development of our project, we gained an understanding of how AI, artificial intelligence, operates and how it depends on the data that is used to train it. We quickly discovered that certain gestures were more difficult for the model to recognize than others. This was due to differences in lighting, positions, or a person’s left or right hand.

Training Our Model

Creating the model through Teachable Machine by Google, allowed us to create four different categories corresponding to a hand gesture we wanted our model to recognize. Each category required a set of images that the model could use to learn the characteristics of that particular gesture. Collecting the training data was one of the most important parts of this project. At the start, our model was able to recognize the thumbs-down pretty well. However, it had some trouble recognizing thumbs up and would classify it as thumbs down. The machine could not recognize that direction as the defining characteristic which separated the two categories. Another interesting observation involved the heart sign gesture. Our model was also able to recognize the heart well. However, there was a problem when we realized that there are multiple ways that people can form a heart using their hands. This meant that successfully recognizing one variation of the gesture did not mean that our model could recognize every other way of creating a heart. We had to consider how different individuals might perform the same gesture and expand our dataset based on that knowledge.

Addressing Limitations of Our Dataset

The primary solution to these challenges that we implemented was to increase the size and diversity of our training image set. By making our dataset larger, we sought to give the machine a better opportunity to identify the characteristics that defined each gesture. One thing we took note of was ensuring that our dataset included gestures performed with both the left and right hands. A machine learning model does not automatically possess the understanding of a left or right hand performing a hand sign. A gesture performed with the left hand may appear visually different from the same gesture performed with the right hand for the model. If one hand is underrepresented in the training data, the model may struggle to recognize gestures performed with that hand. These roadblocks helped us realize that machine learning is an iterative process. Developing an algorithm is not simply a matter of collecting data, training a model, and hoping that it will work correctly. It requires a lot of testing, identifying weaknesses, reconsidering previous assumptions, and making changes based on the results.

Connections to Unmasking AI

One of the main themes from Joy Buolamwini's Unmasking AI was the idea that AI is not inherently neutral. Decisions made by machines are heavily influenced by the data used to train it, assumptions, and biases of the people who develop them. Although our hand classification project is much smaller in scale and does not carry the same consequences as facial analysis, we encountered a similar problem. Our algorithm's ability to recognize different hand gestures relies on whether those gestures were represented in our training data. If our dataset did not include many examples of a specific gesture, the machine was more likely to classify it as something else. Our decision to include both left-handed and right-handed gestures connects directly to this lesson. If we had primarily trained our model using gestures with our right hand, we could have created a system that performed better for individuals using their right hand without meaning to. This would not have been the result of an intentional decision to exclude left-handed individuals. Instead, it would have resulted from overlooking a very important difference among potential users. This shows one of the major concerns presented in Unmasking AI: bias does not always come from bad intentions. It can emerge from assumptions, oversights, and maybe just a simple lack of representation. Developers might believe that their models work well because they perform well under familiar conditions. However, those models may behave differently when exposed to individuals or situations that were not represented during development. Another lesson from Unmasking AI that stood out to us was the importance of holding humans accountable for the systems they create. This is the idea that it can be really easy to blame a machine when it produces an incorrect result. However, our experience demonstrated that the machine's performance was closely connected to the decisions we made throughout the development process. When our model confused thumbs up with thumbs down, it was our responsibility to investigate why these errors were occurring. Rather than simply accepting that the machine was incapable of recognizing these gestures, we reconsidered the data we provided and identified ways to improve our training process.

Conclusion

Overall, our hand classification project provided us with a practical understanding of the challenges involved in developing AI models. We learned that common hand gestures can be difficult for a machine to distinguish and how well a model works depends on the decisions made by humans during its development. If we were to continue with developing our project, we would want to further diversify and increase our dataset by including various individuals with different skin tones, hand sizes, and physical characteristics. We would also want to test the model under different lighting, backgrounds, and camera angles. These other considerations could help our algorithm perform better by implementing a wider range of users and environments. One of the most important lessons we learned is that creating AI is not solely a technical process but also a social and ethical one. Humans developing these algorithms must be very intentional about the data they collect, the assumptions that they make, and the individuals they consider when designing their systems. By acknowledging this, we can work toward creating technology that is more accurate, inclusive, and equitable.
